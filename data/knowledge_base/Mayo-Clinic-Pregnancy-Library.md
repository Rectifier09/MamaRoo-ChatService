# Part 1 — Trimester overviews

## 1st trimester pregnancy: What to expect

### Physical changes

-   Breast tenderness: hormonal shifts make breasts sensitive or sore early on; discomfort usually settles within a few weeks as the body adjusts.

-   Nausea and vomiting: morning sickness commonly appears between weeks 4 and 9 and can strike at any hour.

-   Managing nausea: small frequent meals; bland low-fat foods (bananas, rice, applesauce, toast); lean protein; foods containing ginger; avoid trigger smells; sip cold clear fluids. Seek care if it worsens markedly.

-   Increased urination: blood volume expands, so the kidneys process extra fluid that collects in the bladder.

-   Fatigue: rising progesterone causes exhaustion — rest often, take 15-minute daytime naps, keep diet and exercise balanced.

-   Food cravings and altered taste: hormonal change shifts preferences; ventilate while cooking and hand off odour-producing chores.

-   Heartburn: hormones slow digestion and relax the valve between stomach and oesophagus. Eat small frequent meals, sip drinks separately, avoid fried, citrus and spicy food and chocolate, don't recline after eating, leave time before bed. Safe medicines exist if lifestyle changes aren't enough.

-   Constipation: raised progesterone slows digestion and the growing uterus presses on the bowel. Add fibre, increase fluids, cut caffeine, stay active; ask about stool softeners.

### Emotional changes

-   Pregnancy brings mixed feelings at once — delight, anxiety, excitement, exhaustion.

-   Common worries: the baby's health, adjusting to parenthood, money.

-   Mood swings are normal.

-   Warning sign: get a professional assessment if mood changes last more than two weeks or become intense.

### Prenatal care

-   Providers can be family doctors, obstetricians, nurse-midwives or pregnancy specialists.

-   The first checkup assesses overall health, identifies risk factors, dates the pregnancy, and covers first-trimester screening for chromosomal conditions.

-   Appointments every four weeks through the first 28 weeks; frequency varies with health and history.

-   Any question about pregnancy, childbirth or newborn care is worth raising.

## 2nd trimester pregnancy: What to expect

### Body changes

-   The uterus enlarges and the belly visibly expands; breasts keep growing — wear supportive bras with wide straps, or sports bras.

-   Back pain: hormonal shifts and uterine growth trigger lower back ache. Wear low-heeled shoes with arch support (not high heels, not flats); rest one foot on a stool when standing a long time.

-   Braxton Hicks: mild contractions felt as a slight tightness in the belly that comes and goes; more common in the afternoon or evening, after exercise or after sex. Drink more fluids and urinate often. Get checked if they become regular and intensify.

-   Skin: melasma (brown, tan or grey facial patches) from increased melanin; linea nigra down the abdomen; stretch marks (reddish-brown to purple) on belly, breasts, buttocks and thighs — these cannot be prevented; spider veins on face and legs. Wear sunscreen outdoors. Most changes fade after birth.

-   Nose: extra blood production swells nasal tissue, causing congestion and nosebleeds. Use saline drops or rinses, a humidifier, and petroleum jelly around the nostrils.

-   Teeth and gums: gums bleed more easily when brushing and flossing; vomit acid erodes enamel and raises cavity risk. Keep brushing and flossing.

-   Dizziness: blood flow changes cause lightheadedness. Hydrate, avoid standing still for long, move gradually when changing position, lie on your side if dizzy; support hose may help.

-   Leg cramps: common as pregnancy advances, often at night. Stretch calves before bed, stay active, hydrate, wear low-heeled supportive shoes. Treat by stretching the calf, a hot shower or warm bath, or ice massage.

-   Vaginal discharge: sticky, clear or white fluid is normal. Contact your provider if it has a strong odour, an unusual colour, or comes with pain, soreness or itching.

-   Urinary tract infections are common. Signs: urgency, sharp pain when urinating, cloudy strong-smelling urine, fever, backache. Untreated, they can progress to a kidney infection.

### Emotional and mental health

-   Fatigue usually eases and energy improves; anxiety about labour, delivery and parenting is common.

-   Coping: childbirth classes (including online), healthy lifestyle choices, choosing and researching a paediatric provider, learning about breastfeeding, checking employer parental leave policies and childcare options.

-   Depression and anxiety are common — seek counselling from providers who specialise in pregnancy-related mental health.

### Prenatal care and monitoring

-   Vaccines: COVID-19 if not already given (COVID-19 vaccines don't cause infection with the virus); flu vaccine in autumn and early winter. Household members should be vaccinated too. Vaccination lets the mother make antibodies that protect the baby.

-   Visits focus on fetal growth and screening for maternal health conditions: weight, blood pressure, fundal height (pubic bone to top of the uterus), listening to the heartbeat, and offered ultrasound and other screening tests — with optional sex determination.

-   Virtual visits are an option for low-risk pregnancies; ask what home equipment is needed, such as a blood pressure monitor. Bring a written question list, take detailed notes, and always report concerning symptoms.

## 3rd trimester pregnancy: What to expect

### Physical changes and discomfort

-   Braxton Hicks: irregular tightening that varies in strength and duration; more likely in the afternoon or evening, after activity or sex; increases in frequency and intensity near the due date. Tell your provider if you get more than six an hour with steadily rising intensity — that may be labour.

-   Back pain: hormones relax connective tissue and the uterus stretches abdominal muscles. Use supportive chairs, a foot stool when standing, maternity support garments, heating pads and regular walking. Low heels with arch support; avoid flats and high heels. Severe or persistent pain needs professional care; physical therapy may help.

-   Shortness of breath: pressure under the ribcage as the baby grows; worse lying on the back, so lie on your side. Lung capacity expands, so breathing quickens and fatigue comes sooner. Good posture helps.

-   Heart palpitations: fluttering or racing as blood returns more slowly to the heart; generally not concerning and usually decrease later. Get immediate evaluation if accompanied by chest pain or severe breathlessness.

-   Heartburn and constipation from slowed digestion and compressed intestines. For heartburn: small frequent meals, don't lie down straight after eating, limit fried food, citrus, chocolate, spice, fizzy drinks and tomato-based food. For constipation: more fluids, light activity such as walking, fibre-rich food; don't take over-the-counter stool softeners without approval.

-   Vascular changes: spider veins (tiny red-purplish vessels) on face, neck and arms, usually fading after birth; varicose veins on the legs; haemorrhoids causing pain and itching. Prevent with regular exercise, leg elevation, compression socks, avoiding long standing or crossing legs, and not straining on the toilet. Relief: warm baths, witch hazel pads, dietary fibre, hydration; ask about haemorrhoid creams.

-   Frequent urination as the baby descends into the pelvis; leaking can happen when laughing, coughing, sneezing, bending or lifting. Manage with frequent bathroom breaks, Kegel exercises and panty liners. Contact your provider for clear fluid leakage (possible amniotic fluid) or burning when urinating (possible infection).

### Emotional and psychological health

-   Common concerns: labour pain, how long it will last and whether you can cope; apprehension about parenthood, especially first-time; stress from preparation; disappointment if the baby doesn't arrive by the due date.

-   Coping: journalling, music, talking calmly to the baby, taking pregnancy photographs, attending childbirth education classes.

-   Decisions to make: paediatrician or family doctor for postpartum care; feeding method; circumcision for a male infant.

-   Contact your provider if anxiety is excessive or dominant — counselling or medication may be recommended.

### Prenatal care in the third trimester

-   Checkups become more frequent; visits include weight, blood pressure and a symptom review. Telehealth is an option for low-risk pregnancies — ask what home equipment is needed, prepare written questions and keep notes.

-   Vaccines: COVID-19 is safe and effective in pregnancy and passes antibody protection to the baby (household members should also be vaccinated); Tdap one dose per pregnancy, ideally weeks 27–36, to protect the newborn from whooping cough; RSV vaccine between weeks 32 and 36 during September–January in the US.

-   Screening tests: gestational diabetes; iron deficiency anaemia; Group B strep (vaginal and rectal swabs — it causes no maternal symptoms but risks serious newborn infection, and is treated with antibiotics in labour).

-   Fetal assessment: size and heart rate through the trimester, position in late pregnancy, and a discussion of movement patterns. Your provider will ask about pain management preferences and labour wishes.

-   Birth plan: write down preferences (for example labouring in water, or declining pharmaceutical pain relief), review it with your provider, and stay flexible — circumstances may require changes.

# Part 2 — Fetal development and scans

## Fetal development: The first trimester

### How pregnancy is counted

-   Pregnancy is counted as 40 weeks from the start of the last menstrual period, even though conception happens roughly two weeks later. The first trimester covers the first 12 weeks of that count.

### Week by week

-   Week 3 — fertilisation: sperm and egg merge in a fallopian tube, creating a single cell with 46 chromosomes (23 from each parent). It divides into a cluster resembling a tiny raspberry, called a morula. Multiple eggs or a splitting embryo result in twins.

-   Week 4 — implantation: the morula becomes a blastocyst on reaching the uterus and burrows into the lining. Inner cells become the embryo; outer layers become placental tissue.

-   Week 5 — hormones rise: hCG climbs sharply. Three cell layers emerge — ectoderm (skin, nervous system), mesoderm (heart, bones, organs), endoderm (lungs, intestines). Ovarian hormone production increases and periods stop.

-   Week 6 — neural development: the neural tube along the back is closing; brain and spinal cord develop from it. Arm buds and eye and ear structures appear; the body curves into a C-shape.

-   Week 7 — head formation: brain and face grow rapidly; nostril indentations and retinas become visible; leg buds emerge and arm buds look like paddles.

-   Week 8 — facial features: leg buds become paddle-shaped and fingers begin forming; ears and eyes become noticeable; upper lip and nose form; the trunk straightens. Size: about 1/2 inch (11–14 mm) crown to rump.

-   Week 9 — limbs: arms lengthen and elbows appear; toes emerge; eyelids form. The head is disproportionately large with no distinct chin. Size: slightly under 3/4 inch (16–18 mm).

-   Week 10 — joints: the head rounds; elbows can bend; webbing between fingers and toes recedes and digits lengthen; eyelids and outer ears keep developing.

-   Week 11 — now a fetus: the embryo is officially a fetus. Facial features spread wider; eyelids fused; ears set low. Tooth buds appear; the liver starts making red blood cells; external genitals begin developing. Size: about 2 inches (50 mm); weight about 1/3 ounce (8 g).

-   Week 12 — refinement: fingernails start; the facial profile becomes more recognisable; intestines move into the abdominal cavity. Size: about 2 1/2 inches (61 mm); weight about 1/2 ounce (14 g).

## Fetal development: The 2nd trimester

### Overview

-   Weeks 13–27 (11–25 weeks after conception). Organs, nerves and muscles start functioning; many parents learn the baby's sex and feel movement.

### Weeks 13–16

-   Bones begin hardening, especially skull and limbs; skin thickens from thin and transparent; red blood cells form in the spleen.

-   The baby's sex becomes identifiable; eyes move slowly; ears near their final position; limb movements become coordinated and visible on ultrasound.

-   Week 14: about 3.5 inches, 1.5 ounces. Week 16: about 4.75 inches, 4 ounces.

### Weeks 17–20

-   Toenails start; hearing emerges; the digestive system activates; the baby hiccups, rolls and flips.

-   Vernix caseosa — a greasy, cheese-like coating — begins covering the skin, protecting it from scrapes and hardening. The baby starts passing urine into the amniotic fluid.

-   Week 20 is the halfway point; first movements (quickening) may be felt. Size about 6.3 inches, 11 ounces.

### Weeks 21–27

-   Fine downy lanugo covers the body; eyebrows and visible hair appear by week 22; fingerprints and footprints form; eyelashes and eyebrows form by week 26. Skin smooths as fat accumulates.

-   The sucking reflex develops and the baby may suck a thumb; eye movements become rapid during sleep; the baby responds to familiar voices with movement.

-   Male: testes move toward the scrotum. Female: uterus and ovaries in position, vagina developed.

-   By week 23, some preterm babies can survive outside the uterus.

-   Week 22: about 7.5 inches, 1 pound. Week 26: about 9 inches, nearly 2 pounds. Week 27: the nervous system keeps maturing and fat gain increases.

## Fetal development: The 3rd trimester

### Weeks 28–30

-   Week 28: eyelids can partly open; the central nervous system can regulate body temperature and start breathing movements. About 10 inches crown to rump, around 2.25 pounds.

-   Week 29: coordinated movements — kicking, stretching, grasping.

-   Week 30: eyes open fully; hair growth becomes noticeable; red blood cells begin forming in bone marrow. Over 10.5 inches, approaching 3 pounds.

### Weeks 31–34

-   Week 31: major organ systems complete development; weight gain accelerates.

-   Week 32: lanugo starts shedding; toenails visible. About 11 inches, 3.75 pounds.

-   Week 33: pupils respond to light; skeletal bones harden though the skull stays flexible.

-   Week 34: fingernails reach the fingertips. Nearly 12 inches, over 4.5 pounds.

### Weeks 35–37

-   Week 35: the baby fills most of the amniotic space with limited room, though stretching and rolling continue.

-   Week 36: skin smooths with more fat; limbs look plump. Most babies turn head-down by now; positioning techniques may be suggested around week 37.

-   Week 37: "early term" status; grasp strengthens; the head may descend into the pelvis.

### Weeks 38–40

-   Week 38: head and abdominal measurements become equal; toenails reach the toe tips; most lanugo disappears. Typical weight 6.5 pounds, though some reach 9.

-   Week 39: "full term"; the chest enlarges and fat accumulates for temperature regulation after birth.

-   Week 40: crown-to-rump length averages 14 inches, typical weight 7.5 pounds. The due date is an estimate only — labour varies considerably either side of it.

## Fetal presentation before birth

### Head down, face down (cephalic occiput anterior)

-   The most common birth position and the easiest: the smallest part of the head leads through the birth canal, face down and slightly to one side.

### Head down, face up (cephalic occiput posterior)

-   The head may struggle to pass under the pubic bone, often lengthening labour.

-   Most babies rotate to face-down spontaneously during labour; providers may rotate manually if the second stage stalls.

-   Forceps or vacuum are used more often; C-section is possible.

### Breech presentations (3–4% of pregnancies)

-   Frank breech — knees straight, feet near the head; the most common breech type.

-   Complete breech — both knees bent, legs pulled to the body. Incomplete breech — one or both legs extended below the buttocks; kicking may be felt low in the abdomen.

-   After 36 weeks an external cephalic version may be attempted: one or two members of the care team press on the belly with their hands to turn the baby.

-   If that doesn't work, delivery is usually a planned C-section.

### Transverse lie

-   The baby lies horizontally across the uterus. Common early in pregnancy but rare at the onset of labour.

-   External cephalic version is considered at week 37; C-section usually follows if repositioning fails.

### Twins

-   The first (lower) twin is typically delivered vaginally if head-down.

-   For the second twin: breech vaginal delivery, external cephalic version, or C-section.

-   C-section is indicated for a lower twin that isn't head-down, a large birth weight difference, preterm labour, or patient preference.

## Fetal ultrasound

### Purpose and method

-   Gives parents an early look at the baby and lets providers assess health, growth and development, amniotic fluid levels, placenta position, and screen for complications.

-   A transducer is placed on the abdomen — occasionally inserted vaginally or placed on the perineum — emitting sound waves that convert into a pattern of light and dark areas forming the image.

### Safety

-   Risks are minimal but not zero. Ultrasounds are recommended only for genuine prenatal care reasons under a provider's guidance, not for keepsake purposes.

### What is examined

-   At 11 weeks the head is about half the body's total length.

-   Heart (18–22 weeks): all four chambers and the valves are checked for function and abnormalities.

-   Brain (18–22 weeks): the cerebellum's shape is assessed for neural tube defects such as spina bifida, where part of the neural tube doesn't develop or close correctly.

-   Head measurements help date the pregnancy; the white circular outline is the skull, with the midline dividing the hemispheres.

-   Limbs: open hands and fingers indicate normal development. The femur is the largest bone and easily measured; the lower leg has the tibia and fibula.

-   Eyes: rapid eye movements begin around 23 weeks; eyelids start opening at 28 weeks.

-   Spine: cervical spine and full spine are among the most recognisable structures.

-   Umbilical cord: the attachment site is checked for omphalocele (an opening at the navel) and gastroschisis (a break in abdominal wall tissue).

### 3D ultrasound

-   Produces clearer, more detailed images than standard 2D, useful for facial abnormalities, skeletal problems or neural tube defects. Elective 3D scans without medical need are advised against.

# Part 3 — Prenatal care and testing

## Prenatal care: First trimester visits

### Medical history

-   Providers review menstrual patterns and prior pregnancies; family and personal medical history; toxic exposures; all medicines, supplements and vitamins; tobacco, alcohol, caffeine and drug use; and travel to regions with malaria, tuberculosis, Zika or mpox.

-   Candid discussion of sensitive topics such as domestic abuse or substance use history helps providers give better care.

### Due date

-   The estimated date is 40 weeks from the first day of the last period — not a prediction of the birth date.

-   Calculation: add seven days to the start of the last period, then count back three months.

-   Early ultrasound may adjust the date if the discrepancy exceeds seven days.

### Physical examination

-   Weight and height for BMI; breast examination; pelvic examination; Pap test if due; heart, lung and thyroid checks as needed.

### Laboratory tests

-   Blood type and Rh status (special care if Rh negative).

-   Haemoglobin to screen for anaemia.

-   Immunity screening for rubella and varicella.

-   Infectious disease testing: hepatitis B, syphilis, gonorrhoea, chlamydia and HIV.

-   Urinalysis for urinary tract infection.

### Screening and counselling

-   Prenatal genetic screening using ultrasound or blood tests is typically offered, for conditions such as Down syndrome.

-   Lifestyle counselling covers nutrition, prenatal vitamins, exercise, sex, dental care, vaccinations, workplace safety, medicines in pregnancy and stopping smoking.

### Common discomforts and follow-up

-   Breast tenderness and swelling are frequent. Morning sickness with or without vomiting is common; severe cases need medical consultation.

-   Follow-up visits every four weeks. Between 12 and 14 weeks a Doppler lets you hear the heartbeat; ultrasound may also be offered.

## Prenatal care: Second trimester visits

### Frequency and basic monitoring

-   Appointments about every four weeks; blood pressure and weight at each visit.

### Tracking fetal development

-   Fundal height (pubic bone to top of uterus) is measured at each visit. After 20 weeks the number in centimetres often matches the number of weeks pregnant, plus or minus 2 to 3 centimetres.

-   A Doppler amplifies the heartbeat at second-trimester visits.

-   Fetal movements — flutters or kicks — typically begin between 18 and 22 weeks; timing varies, so tell your provider when movement starts.

### Testing options

-   Genetic screening: blood tests screen for chromosome and gene conditions including spina bifida and Down syndrome. Abnormal results usually lead to diagnostic testing by chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis.

-   Ultrasound gives developmental information and can reveal the baby's sex if wanted.

-   Blood work at weeks 24–28: complete blood count and iron levels; gestational diabetes screening; Rh status and antibody testing for Rh-negative individuals.

### Communication

-   No health-related question is too small to raise during this period.

## Prenatal care: Third trimester visits

### Frequency and monitoring

-   Visits every 2–4 weeks depending on health and history; from week 36, every 1–2 weeks until delivery.

-   Providers check blood pressure, weight, the baby's heartbeat and fetal movements.

### Fetal movement tracking

-   Monitor daily movement counts — the baby should move at least 10 times within a two-hour window.

-   Contact your care team if movement decreases significantly as pregnancy progresses.

### Vaccinations

-   Flu, COVID-19 and RSV are recommended.

-   Tdap is particularly important between weeks 27 and 36.

### Group B streptococcus

-   Tested in the third trimester via vaginal and anal swabs.

-   A positive result means intravenous antibiotics during labour to prevent newborn infection.

-   Anyone with a previous baby affected by GBS disease also receives preventive antibiotics.

### Baby's position

-   Providers check for head-down positioning, optimal for vaginal delivery.

-   Breech types include frank, footling and complete.

-   External cephalic version — a procedure to move the baby to a headfirst position — may be offered after week 36 if breech persists; otherwise a planned caesarean typically follows.

### Preparation

-   Discuss labour signs, pain management, birth plan preferences and sex with your care team.

-   Agree contact protocols and know when to seek immediate attention — vaginal bleeding or fluid leakage.

## Prenatal testing: Is it right for me?

### Two categories

-   Screening tests estimate risk; diagnostic tests confirm a diagnosis.

### Screening options

-   First trimester screening: blood work plus an ultrasound measuring nuchal translucency — the clear space at the back of the baby's neck. An enlarged measurement may indicate Down syndrome or other genetic conditions.

-   Second trimester screening: a quad screen blood test measuring four substances, assessing risk for Down syndrome, trisomy 18 and neural tube defects. A routine anatomy ultrasound is recommended for all pregnancies in this period.

-   Cell-free DNA screening: can be done in any trimester; analyses baby DNA circulating in the pregnant person's blood, identifying risk for conditions such as Down syndrome and trisomy 18, plus fetal sex.

### Diagnostic testing

-   Reserved for higher-risk situations. Gives definitive answers but carries a slight miscarriage risk. Examples: chorionic villus sampling and amniocentesis.

### Questions to consider first

-   Would the results change decisions about the pregnancy or prenatal care planning?

-   Can you tolerate the anxiety of a possible false result? False positives show risk where none exists; false negatives miss real risk.

### Key point

-   Testing is entirely optional. Genetic counsellors can help evaluate options, accuracy rates, and weigh specific risks against the value of the information.

## Prenatal vitamins: Why they matter, how to choose

### Why they matter

-   Folic acid prevents neural tube defects affecting brain and spinal cord development. Ideally start at least three months before conception.

-   Iron supports placenta and fetal development by enabling blood production to carry oxygen, and helps prevent maternal anaemia.

### Choosing one

-   Available over the counter at most pharmacies; providers may recommend specific brands.

-   Look for folic acid, iron, calcium and vitamin D for bone and teeth development.

-   Useful additions: vitamin C, vitamin A, vitamin E, B vitamins, zinc and iodine.

-   Avoid excessive doses — high concentrations of some vitamins, particularly vitamin A, can harm fetal development.

### Special circumstances

-   Higher folic acid doses (4 mg / 4,000 mcg) may be recommended after a previous pregnancy affected by a neural tube defect.

-   Omega-3 supplements may help if dietary fish intake is insufficient.

### Timing

-   Start before conception where possible — neural tube development happens in the first month, potentially before you know you are pregnant.

### Side effects

-   For constipation: more fluids, more fibre, approved physical activity, and ask about stool softeners.

## Fundal height: An accurate sign of fetal growth?

-   Fundal height is the distance in centimetres from the pubic bone to the top of the uterus, used to estimate the baby's size.

-   After week 24 it generally matches the number of weeks of pregnancy, plus or minus 3 centimetres — at 27 weeks, expect roughly 27 centimetres.

-   Accuracy is lower in people with obesity, complicated by a history of uterine fibroids, and decreases after 36 weeks.

-   Unusual measurements may indicate fetal growth restriction, multiple fetuses, oligohydramnios (too little amniotic fluid) or polyhydramnios (too much).

-   Providers may order an ultrasound if measurements deviate, to identify the cause and monitor more closely.

-   It is only a tool for estimating growth, not an exact science — but steady measurements are a reassuring sign.

# Part 4 — Diet, weight and exercise

## Pregnancy diet: Focus on these essential nutrients

### Folate and folic acid

-   Purpose: prevents neural tube defects affecting brain and spinal cord.

-   Daily: 400 mcg pre-conception; 600–1,000 mcg during pregnancy.

-   Sources: fortified breakfast cereals (100–400 mcg per serving), dark leafy greens such as spinach (131 mcg per half cup cooked), citrus fruits, legumes, asparagus (89 mcg per 4 spears).

-   Start prenatal vitamins at least three months before conception.

### Calcium

-   Purpose: bone and tooth development; nerve and muscle function.

-   Daily: 1,000 mg for adults; 1,300 mg for pregnant teenagers.

-   Sources: dairy (milk about 300 mg per cup), fortified juices and cereals (up to 349 mg per cup), broccoli and kale, canned salmon with bones (181 mg per 3 ounces).

### Vitamin D

-   Purpose: works with calcium to build bones and teeth.

-   Daily: 600 International Units.

-   Sources: fatty fish such as salmon (570 IU per 3 ounces cooked), fortified milk and juices (100 IU per serving), eggs (43.5 IU per large boiled egg).

### Protein

-   Purpose: fetal growth and development.

-   Daily: 71 grams.

-   Sources: lean meat and poultry (26 g per 3 ounces of chicken breast), fish (17 g per 3 ounces of salmon), legumes and nuts, cottage cheese (28 g per cup), eggs and soy products.

### Iron

-   Purpose: produces haemoglobin to deliver oxygen to fetal tissues; prevents anaemia.

-   Daily: 27 mg — double the non-pregnant requirement.

-   Sources: fortified breakfast cereals (up to 20 mg per half cup), lean red meat (3 mg per 3 ounces), spinach, legumes, poultry.

-   Pair iron with vitamin C to enhance absorption; avoid taking it with calcium-fortified juices.

-   Deficiency raises the risk of premature birth, low birth weight and postpartum depression.

### Supplements

-   Prenatal vitamins usually contain the necessary iron.

-   Providers may recommend extra supplementation for strict vegetarians or those with chronic conditions.

-   Consult a doctor before any herbal supplement — some can harm fetal development.

## Pregnancy nutrition: Foods to avoid during pregnancy

### High-mercury seafood

-   Excess mercury harms fetal nervous system development. The FDA advises against bigeye tuna, king mackerel, marlin, orange roughy, swordfish, shark and tilefish.

-   Safe alternatives: anchovies, catfish, cod, light canned tuna, salmon, shrimp, tilapia. Recommended intake 8–12 ounces weekly (2–3 servings).

### Raw and undercooked seafood

-   Sushi, sashimi, ceviche and oysters carry bacterial and viral risk.

-   Refrigerated smoked seafood (lox, nova style, kippered) should be avoided unless cooked into a hot dish.

-   Cook fish to 145°F (63°C) until opaque and flaking easily. Shellfish must turn milky-white, with shells opening during cooking.

### Undercooked meat, poultry and eggs

-   Pregnancy raises susceptibility to foodborne illness with potential fetal consequences.

-   Cook all meat and poultry thoroughly, verified with a thermometer.

-   Hot dogs and lunch meats carry listeria risk — heat until steaming.

-   Eggs must be fully cooked; avoid foods with raw or partly cooked egg, including homemade dressings, eggnog and tiramisu.

### Unpasteurised dairy

-   May harbour harmful pathogens. Soft cheeses (brie, feta, blue cheese) only if labelled pasteurised. Avoid unpasteurised juice and cider entirely.

### Unwashed produce and raw sprouts

-   Wash all raw fruit and vegetables thoroughly.

-   Raw sprouts — alfalfa, clover, radish, mung bean — may carry dangerous bacteria and must be cooked through.

### Caffeine

-   Keep below 200 mg daily. An 8-ounce cup of brewed coffee has about 95 mg; tea about 47 mg per 8 ounces.

### Herbal tea

-   Not much is known about the effects of certain herbs on fetuses, so herbal teas are inadvisable unless your provider approves.

### Alcohol

-   No amount of alcohol has been proved safe during pregnancy. It raises miscarriage and stillbirth risk and can cause fetal alcohol syndrome.

## Pregnancy and fish: What's safe to eat?

### Benefits

-   Seafood provides protein, iron and zinc. Omega-3s, particularly DHA, support brain development.

### Mercury

-   Large predatory fish accumulate mercury that can damage a developing brain and nervous system. Mercury can build up in the bloodstream over time if high-mercury fish is eaten frequently.

### How much

-   8 to 12 ounces weekly of lower-mercury seafood, across two to three servings, per FDA, EPA and dietary guidelines.

### Best choices

-   Salmon, anchovies, herring, sardines, freshwater trout and Pacific mackerel — good omega-3 profile, minimal mercury.

-   Also safe: shrimp, pollock, tilapia, cod, catfish and canned light tuna.

-   Limit white (albacore) tuna and tuna steaks to 6 ounces weekly.

### Avoid completely

-   Shark, swordfish, king mackerel and tilefish.

### Preparation

-   Cook seafood to 145°F internal temperature.

-   Avoid raw, undercooked or cold-smoked fish, including sushi and refrigerated preparations labelled nova style, lox or jerky.

-   Check local water advisories; limit locally caught fish to 6 ounces weekly if no guidance is available.

### Alternative omega-3 sources

-   Flaxseed, canola oil, walnuts, sunflower seeds, edamame, and fortified dairy and eggs. Prenatal supplements with DHA exist, though researchers haven't yet determined whether supplements promote brain development.

## Pregnancy weight gain: What's healthy?

### Targets by pre-pregnancy BMI

-   Underweight (BMI under 18.5): 28–40 pounds (about 13–18 kg).

-   Normal weight (BMI 18.5–24.9): 25–35 pounds (about 11–16 kg).

-   Overweight (BMI 25–29.9): 15–25 pounds (about 7–11 kg).

-   Obese (BMI 30 or above): 11–20 pounds (about 5–9 kg).

### Multiples

-   Substantially higher. With a normal BMI, 37–54 pounds (17–25 kg) versus 25–35 for a single baby.

### Risks of obesity in pregnancy

-   Gestational diabetes, preeclampsia, caesarean delivery, premature birth, miscarriage and birth defects.

### Where the weight goes

-   Fetal weight 7–8 pounds, plus breast tissue, uterine enlargement, placenta, amniotic fluid, increased blood volume, fluid retention and maternal fat stores of 6–8 pounds.

### First trimester

-   Minimal gain — roughly 1–4 pounds for those starting at a healthy weight, which fits with managing early nausea.

### Second and third trimesters

-   Steady weekly gain matters. Healthy starting weight: about one pound weekly. Overweight or obese: about half a pound weekly, needing roughly 300 extra calories a day.

### Exercise and nutrition

-   Moderate-intensity activity for thirty minutes on most days — brisk walking or swimming — alongside nutrient-dense food.

## Pregnancy exercises

### Benefits and precautions

-   Consistent activity reduces discomfort and helps manage weight gain. Combining aerobic work with muscle strengthening prepares the body for labour and supports recovery afterwards.

-   Consult your provider before starting, particularly with a high-risk pregnancy or complications. Move slowly to stay stable and avoid falls, and stop anything that makes you feel unsteady.

### Core-strengthening exercises

-   Wall pushups — chest and arms. Face a wall, hands at shoulder height slightly wider than shoulder width, lower the chest by bending the elbows, keeping the spine aligned. Build toward 15 repetitions.

-   Squats with a fitness ball — prepares the pelvis for labour positioning. Ball behind the back against a wall, feet shoulder-width apart, slide down to 90 degrees at the knees with heels grounded. Adjust depth as needed and have someone nearby. Build toward 10.

-   Leg raises — back and abdomen. On hands and knees with straight arms, extend one leg behind, parallel to the floor, then alternate. Build toward 10 per side.

-   Step-ups — use a step stool or stairs with a wall or railing for balance. Lead with one foot, alternating the leading foot each repetition, staying upright.

-   Modified side plank — balance and lateral trunk muscles. Lie on your side, up on the forearm with the shoulder directly above the elbow, body aligned. Hold several seconds; build toward 10 per side.

### Balance trainer exercises

-   Supported V-sits — back against the balance trainer, feet flat, arms extended; lift one foot until the leg is parallel to the floor, hold, return. Build toward 10 per leg.

-   V-sits on the balance trainer — sit upright on the trainer, feet flat, arms forward; lean back until you feel the abdominals engage, hold, return. Build toward 10.

-   One-leg V-sits — the same with one foot lifted; an advanced variation. Build toward 10 per side.

### Resistance tubing

-   Seated rowing — upper back. Sit upright on a fitness ball or sturdy chair, feet hip-width apart, tubing under the feet; pull the handles back, drawing the shoulder blades together. Build toward 15.

-   Seated dead lifts — lower back. Sit upright with tubing under the feet, lean forward from the hips toward the thighs, return upright. Wrap tubing round the hands for more resistance. Build toward 15.

### Progression

-   Starting slow and gradually adding repetitions each day works well. Listen to your body and adjust for comfort and fatigue.

## Pregnancy stretches

### Guidance

-   Regular stretching eases common aches. Work up to 10 repetitions daily.

-   Get medical clearance first — some complications make stretching inadvisable. Talk to your health care team to make sure these stretches are safe for you.

### The six stretches

-   Low back stretch — on hands and knees with head and back aligned, pull the stomach in and round the back slightly, hold briefly, relax.

-   Backward stretch — from hands and knees with straight arms and hands under the shoulders, curl backward toward the heels while tucking the head, hold several seconds. Targets back, pelvis and thighs.

-   Backward stretch with a fitness ball — kneel, place hands on the ball, curl slowly back toward the heels to a comfortable range, hold briefly.

-   Standing pelvic tilt — stand against a wall, feet shoulder-width apart, push the lower back against the wall and hold.

-   Pelvic tilt with a fitness ball — seated against the ball with feet flat, push the lower back upward and hold. Have a spotter nearby.

-   Torso rotation — sit cross-legged, hold the right foot with the left hand, place the right hand behind you and gently twist the upper body to the right. Repeat on the other side.

## Iron deficiency anemia during pregnancy

### Cause

-   Blood volume expands significantly, raising iron requirements. Iron makes haemoglobin, which carries oxygen through red blood cells to fetal tissues. Low stores or intake trigger deficiency.

### Risks

-   Premature delivery before 37 weeks; low birth weight; higher risk of infant death around birth; increased likelihood of postpartum depression.

### Who is at higher risk

-   Pregnancies spaced closely together; carrying multiples; severe morning sickness; inadequate iron intake; historically heavy periods; pre-existing anaemia.

### Symptoms

-   Extreme tiredness, weakness, dizziness, headaches, pale skin, yellowish skin or eyes, breathlessness. Ice chewing (pica) may occur.

-   Severe cases: rapid heartbeat, low blood pressure, difficulty concentrating.

### Prevention

-   27 mg of iron daily. Prenatal vitamins usually contain enough; separate supplements may be recommended.

-   Animal sources (most absorbable): lean red meat, poultry, fish.

-   Plant sources: fortified cereals, leafy greens, legumes.

-   Pair with vitamin C foods such as orange juice or strawberries; avoid calcium-fortified juices at the same time.

### Treatment

-   If anaemia persists despite prenatal vitamins, further testing identifies the cause. More iron may be needed; intravenous iron is available for those who can't tolerate tablets or have had certain surgery.

# Part 5 — Symptoms and discomforts

## Back pain during pregnancy: 7 tips for relief

### Why it happens

-   Weight gain shifts the centre of gravity forward, straining the spine; abdominal muscles that support the spine stretch and weaken; hormones relax connective tissue in the pelvic joints, reducing stability.

### The seven strategies

-   Posture: stand tall with the chest lifted and shoulders relaxed, knees slightly bent rather than locked, feet wide for balance. Sit in chairs with back support or use a lumbar pillow. Change position often when standing for long periods.

-   Footwear and support garments: low-heeled shoes with arch support beat flats or high heels. Abdominal support garments and pregnancy binders redistribute weight away from the back. Pelvic support belts may help, though the evidence is mixed.

-   Lifting: bend at the knees, not the waist; keep the back straight and feet wide; lift with the legs; hold objects close. Know your limits and ask for help.

-   Sleep position: side-sleeping is better than back or stomach. Use pregnancy pillows between the knees, under the stomach and behind the back; a full-length body pillow also works.

-   Heat, cold and massage: heating pads, warm bottles or ice packs give temporary relief. Use low heat and wrap the source in a towel to prevent burns. Massage may comfort, though long-term benefit is limited.

-   Stay active: continuing pre-pregnancy exercise strengthens the back. Walking, running and water exercise remain safe. Prenatal yoga and stretching classes help; physical therapy addresses persistent pain.

-   Complementary therapies: acupuncture shows promise when given by trained practitioners, and chiropractic adjustment by licensed specialists may help. Always consult your provider first.

### Lower back stretch

-   On hands and knees, pull the stomach in while rounding the back slightly, hold several seconds, then relax. Build to ten repetitions.

### Warning signs

-   Contact your provider immediately if back pain comes with vaginal bleeding, fever, cramping or contractions, or burning when urinating.

-   Call if pain lasts longer than two weeks or follows a fall or injury. Back pain can indicate preterm labour or a urinary tract infection.

## Headaches during pregnancy: What's the best treatment?

### Without medication

-   Track headache patterns in a journal to identify and avoid triggers — foods, odours or others.

-   Daily moderate aerobic exercise such as walking.

-   Manage stress through talking with friends or hobbies; see a mental health professional if overwhelmed.

-   Relaxation: deep breathing, meditation, yoga, or prenatal massage lying on your side rather than face-down.

-   Regular meals with balanced nutrition and adequate hydration.

-   Consistent sleep schedule — insufficient rest contributes to headaches.

-   Biofeedback training to control functions like heart rate and breathing; ask your provider for a referral.

### Medication

-   Acetaminophen (paracetamol/Tylenol) is generally considered safe for occasional use.

-   Providers may suggest other options — always consult before starting any treatment.

### Warning signs

-   Severe pain intensity, prolonged duration, or headaches potentially related to high blood pressure or other pregnancy conditions.

## Nausea during pregnancy: A good thing?

-   Research indicates that people with nausea and vomiting in the first trimester have a reduced miscarriage risk compared with those without.

-   The body produces hCG shortly after fertilisation, which may trigger nausea and vomiting; higher levels correlate with more severe sickness, notably in hyperemesis gravidarum.

-   Those carrying multiples typically have higher hCG and more nausea. Rising oestrogen also intensifies it.

-   High pregnancy hormone levels aren't always associated with nausea and vomiting, however.

-   Some evidence suggests placental growth rather than hormones alone may trigger symptoms.

-   Absence of morning sickness does not indicate a problem — many pregnancies progress normally without it.

## Leg cramps during pregnancy: Preventable?

### Overview

-   Painful contractions in the calf, foot or both; most frequent at night in the second and third trimesters. The exact cause is unclear.

### Prevention

-   Stretching: stretch the calves before bed. Stand facing a wall at arm's length, hands on the wall, one foot behind the other; bend the forward leg while keeping the back leg straight with the heel on the floor. Hold about 30 seconds with a straight back and hips facing forward.

-   Physical activity may reduce cramps — check with your provider before starting.

-   Hydration: monitor urine colour — clear or light yellow means well hydrated; darker yellow means not enough water.

-   Nutrition: 1,000 mg of calcium daily during pregnancy or when pregnancy is possible. Magnesium supplementation has mixed evidence; magnesium-rich foods include whole grains, beans, dried fruit, nuts and seeds.

-   Footwear: comfortable shoes with proper arch support and firm heel counters that stabilise the foot.

### During a cramp

-   Stretch the affected calf immediately; walk, then elevate the legs while sitting to prevent recurrence; apply heat via a hot shower or warm bath; try ice massage or muscle massage.

### When to seek help

-   Consult your provider if cramps persist or become severe.

## Ankle swelling during pregnancy

### Causes

-   The body retains more fluid; the growing uterus presses on veins, restricting blood return to the heart; hormonal changes contribute.

### Management

-   Rest and elevation: avoid standing for long, lie down with legs raised or sit with feet elevated, and do ankle circles and calf stretches while seated.

-   Sleep on your left side to relieve pressure on the inferior vena cava.

-   Compression wear: supportive tights or stockings during the day.

-   Water activities: standing or walking in a pool compresses leg tissue.

-   Clothing: avoid tight bands around ankles or calves.

-   Hydration: about 10 cups (2.3 litres) of fluid daily.

### When to seek help

-   Sudden painful swelling, especially in one leg — possible deep vein thrombosis.

-   Rapidly worsening swelling.

-   Sudden facial or hand swelling — these may indicate gestational diabetes or pre-eclampsia.

### Prognosis

-   Mild foot and ankle swelling usually resolves within weeks of delivery.

## Hemorrhoids during pregnancy: What's the best treatment?

### What they are

-   Enlarged blood vessels around the anus and lower rectum; common in pregnancy and usually manageable conservatively.

### Relief

-   Sit in a tub filled with warm water, without soaps or bath products.

-   Apply witch hazel-soaked pads to the anal area.

-   Ask your provider about pregnancy-safe haemorrhoid creams or suppositories.

-   Use a ring cushion or doughnut pillow to reduce pressure when sitting.

-   Limit prolonged sitting, which increases venous pressure; take regular movement and standing breaks.

### Treating the underlying constipation

-   More dietary fibre from fruit and vegetables; pregnancy-safe fibre supplements under medical guidance; approved stool softeners; adequate fluids; daily physical activity.

### When to seek care

-   Contact your provider if symptoms worsen. Surgery may be considered after delivery if conservative measures fail.

## Pregnancy constipation: Are stool softeners safe?

-   Docusate sodium (Colace) and docusate calcium (Surfak) are generally considered safe during pregnancy, with minimal absorption.

-   Also approved: bulk-forming laxatives such as psyllium and polycarbophil (not absorbed); saline laxatives including magnesium hydroxide and magnesium citrate; stimulant laxatives such as bisacodyl; and lactulose.

-   Always consult your provider before taking any medicine, including over-the-counter softeners and laxatives.

-   Prevention: more fluids, particularly water and prune juice; regular physical activity; high-fibre foods (fruit, vegetables, beans, whole grains); fibre supplements with medical approval.

-   Iron supplements can worsen constipation — tell your care team if you're taking iron, as a stool softener may be needed.

-   Pregnancy constipation means fewer than three bowel movements a week and is common.

## Pregnancy acne: What's the best treatment?

### Cause

-   Excess sebum production triggered by hormonal change.

### Safe self-care

-   Wash the face twice daily with mild soap and warm water, using hands only.

-   Avoid facial scrubs, astringents and over-washing, which irritate the skin.

-   Shampoo daily if acne appears along the hairline; keep hair clean and off the face.

-   Don't pick or squeeze blemishes — risk of infection or scarring.

-   Use only water-based or non-comedogenic products.

-   Avoid tight clothing and hats that trap sweat and oil.

### Safe medications

-   Erythromycin (Erygel, Erythra-Derm) and clindamycin (Cleocin T, Clindagel and others).

-   Benzoyl peroxide treatments.

-   Azelaic acid products.

### To avoid

-   Oral isotretinoin (Amnesteem, Claravis) — known to cause birth defects.

-   Topical retinoids, despite minimal skin absorption.

-   Oily or greasy cosmetics and sunscreens.

### Recommendation

-   Consult a provider to weigh benefits and risks of specific treatments.

## Vulvar varicosities during pregnancy: What can you do?

### Cause

-   Varicose veins on the vulva from increased blood flow to the pelvis; blood returns more slowly from the lower body and pools. May occur alongside leg varicose veins.

### Symptoms

-   Not always symptomatic. Possible fullness, pressure, swelling and discomfort.

-   Rare severe cases show bluish, bumpy bulging vessels.

-   Worsened by exercise, sex, and prolonged standing or sitting.

### Relief

-   Get a support garment designed for the condition — ask your provider for a recommendation.

-   Change position frequently; avoid long periods standing or sitting.

-   Elevate the legs to improve circulation.

-   Apply cold compresses to the area.

### Delivery and recovery

-   These veins carry minimal blood flow, so bleeding during vaginal delivery is generally manageable, and they rarely interfere with delivery.

-   Vulvar varicosities that form during pregnancy go away by about six weeks after delivery.

## Implantation bleeding: Common in early pregnancy?

-   Light spotting or minimal bleeding around 10 to 14 days after conception, when the fertilised egg embeds in the uterine lining.

-   Common in early pregnancy and generally not a problem. Timing often coincides with the expected period.

-   Lighter than a normal period and resolves on its own without treatment. Some people notice it, others don't.

-   It can be mistaken for a light period, which may throw off due date calculations — first-trimester ultrasound establishes accurate dating.

-   If you're concerned about any vaginal bleeding during pregnancy, contact your health care provider.

# Part 6 — Medicines and safety

## Medicine concerns during pregnancy

### How medicines affect pregnancy

-   Factors: stage of pregnancy, medication strength and dose, whether the placenta lets the substance through, individual genetic response, and overall maternal health.

-   Effects range from none, to direct fetal damage, to reduced placental blood flow, impaired placental function, or triggering contractions.

### Amphetamines

-   Prescribed stimulants such as dextroamphetamine treat ADHD and narcolepsy.

-   Methamphetamine use carries serious risks including miscarriage, low birth weight, preterm birth and placental separation. Exposed newborns may have withdrawal symptoms and brain development problems.

-   Avoid methamphetamine entirely; discuss the benefits and risks of prescribed amphetamines with your provider.

### Benzodiazepines

-   Treat anxiety, insomnia and seizures. Birth defects are unlikely, but risks include miscarriage, premature delivery and low birth weight.

-   Third-trimester use may cause neonatal withdrawal with tremors, poor feeding and breathing difficulty.

### Gabapentinoids

-   Gabapentin and pregabalin, for seizures and nerve pain, show a slightly elevated heart defect risk and preterm birth. Increased folic acid may be recommended. More research is needed.

### Opioids

-   Cross the placenta, causing fetal dependence and neonatal withdrawal — diarrhoea, fever, high-pitched crying and seizures. Birth defects are rare, but risks include miscarriage and poor fetal growth.

### Medications for opioid use disorder

-   Methadone and buprenorphine are preferred, with no birth defect associations, and improve maternal outcomes by reducing overdose risk and supporting recovery. Dose adjustments during pregnancy are common. Neonatal withdrawal risk exists but is lower than with illicit opioids.

### Triptans for migraine

-   Recent research confirms these are safe during pregnancy. Sumatriptan has the most established safety data and is the preferred choice.

### SSRIs

-   First-line for depression and anxiety, with low birth defect risk. Benefits generally outweigh risks — stopping an effective psychiatric medicine increases relapse. Sertraline is typically preferred for new treatment.

### Cannabis

-   Both medical and recreational use should be avoided. Risks include gestational diabetes, hypertension disorders, low birth weight, preterm birth and placental complications.

### Nicotine replacement therapy

-   Safer than smoking; delivers nicotine in lower, slower-release doses. Use the minimum effective dose and discuss risks and benefits.

### Questions for your provider

-   Ask about adjusting medicines when planning pregnancy, effects on the pregnancy, what to avoid, dosage changes, breastfeeding compatibility, and what is appropriate during pregnancy.

## Antibiotics and pregnancy: What's safe?

### General

-   Safety depends on the specific medicine, the trimester, treatment duration and dose.

-   Providers prescribe antibiotics in pregnancy when necessary, including routinely during caesarean deliveries.

### Considered safe

-   Penicillins — amoxicillin and ampicillin.

-   Cephalosporins — cefaclor and cephalexin.

-   Clindamycin.

-   Metronidazole for certain infections.

### Avoid or use with caution

-   Tetracyclines can affect bone growth and cause a baby's teeth to discolour; should not be used after the fifth week of gestation.

-   Nitrofurantoin may increase cleft lip risk in the first trimester, but is safe in the second and third.

-   Macrolides — erythromycin, clarithromycin and azithromycin — have been associated with heart rhythm issues and premature birth, though azithromycin may be safe for specific infections needing treatment.

## Allergy medications and pregnancy: What's safe?

### Non-medication approaches first

-   Minimise contact with triggers.

-   Saline nasal spray as often as needed.

-   Nasal irrigation with a neti pot using sterile or distilled water.

-   Regular physical activity to reduce nasal inflammation.

-   Adhesive nasal strips to keep passages open.

### Mild symptoms

-   Loratadine (Claritin, Alavert) or cetirizine (Zyrtec Allergy).

### Moderate to severe

-   An antihistamine combined with a nasal steroid spray: budesonide (Rhinocort Allergy), fluticasone (Flonase Allergy Relief) or mometasone (Nasonex 24HR Allergy). Use the lowest effective dose.

### Decongestants

-   Oxymetazoline nasal spray: maximum 3 consecutive days.

-   Pseudoephedrine (Sudafed): 30 to 60 milligrams every 4 to 6 hours in the second and third trimesters only, maximum 240 mg daily. Avoid in the first trimester due to possible birth defect risk.

### Key point

-   Always consult your provider before starting any allergy medicine.

## Antidepressants: Safe during pregnancy?

### Why treatment matters

-   Untreated depression raises risks for mother and baby: premature birth, low birth weight, restricted fetal growth, postpartum depression and impaired bonding. Untreated, people may neglect prenatal care and nutrition.

### Safety overview

-   Antidepressants can be appropriate when benefits outweigh risks. Overall birth defect risk is very low for most medicines; decisions should involve careful discussion about individual circumstances and choice of drug.

### SSRIs

-   Generally considered safe: citalopram, sertraline, escitalopram, fluoxetine. Most studies show no association with birth defects.

-   Paroxetine should be avoided due to possible increased heart defect risk in the first trimester.

### SNRIs

-   Duloxetine and venlafaxine are possible choices, but may raise blood pressure — monitoring is needed.

### Secondary options

-   Bupropion: limited first-line use; potential links to miscarriage or heart defects.

-   Tricyclics: usually reserved for treatment-resistant cases; clomipramine may increase birth defect risk.

### Potential risks

-   Neonatal withdrawal after late-pregnancy exposure: irritability, tremors, feeding difficulty and respiratory distress, lasting up to two weeks.

-   Autism spectrum association remains unclear; most research indicates minimal risk.

-   Venlafaxine and amitriptyline may increase gestational diabetes risk.

### Clinical guidance

-   Prioritise maternal mental health stability. Stopping medication risks relapse, particularly in severe depression. Cognitive behavioural therapy and exercise may provide additional support.

## Aspirin during pregnancy: Is it safe?

### Low dose

-   Low-dose aspirin (81 mg) is considered safe throughout pregnancy and may be recommended in specific situations.

-   Indications: blood clotting disorders, history of preeclampsia, multiple previous miscarriages or losses, multiple pregnancy, kidney disease, diabetes or high blood pressure.

### Higher doses — risks by trimester

-   First trimester: elevated miscarriage risk and potential congenital defects.

-   Second trimester: FDA guidance recommends avoiding aspirin, ibuprofen and naproxen after 19 weeks — risk of rare but serious fetal kidney complications and reduced amniotic fluid.

-   Third trimester: premature closure of fetal heart vessels; increased bleeding risk in premature infant brains with prolonged high-dose use. Frequent monitoring is required if therapy continues.

### Alternative

-   Acetaminophen (paracetamol/Tylenol) may be a safer option for pain relief. Consult your provider before taking any medicine.

## Birth control pills: Harmful in early pregnancy?

-   Taking oral contraceptives in early pregnancy does not appear to increase the likelihood of birth defects.

-   Some research has suggested possible links to low birth weight, premature delivery or newborn urinary tract issues, but providers have generally not seen these in practice.

-   Birth control pills reduce the overall risk of pregnancy and specifically lower the chance of ectopic pregnancy.

-   Exception: with the progestin-only minipill, any pregnancy that does occur carries a slightly higher chance of being ectopic, typically in the fallopian tubes.

-   If you suspect pregnancy, take a home test and stop the pill if positive. If no test is available, stop the pill until you see a provider and use an alternative such as condoms.

-   If you're worried about having taken pills before discovering the pregnancy, speak to your provider — the actual risk of birth defects is minimal.

## Vaccines during pregnancy: Are they safe?

### Recommended

-   Flu shot (inactivated influenza vaccine): made from killed virus, safe during flu season. Avoid the nasal spray version, which contains live virus.

-   Tdap: one dose each pregnancy, ideally between weeks 27 and 36, protecting the newborn from whooping cough.

-   COVID-19: updated doses recommended regardless of prior vaccination. Research confirms safety for pregnant people and babies. Boosters also recommended when appropriate.

-   RSV vaccine (Abrysvo): recommended at 32–36 weeks during autumn and winter when the virus circulates most.

### To avoid

-   Chickenpox (varicella) vaccine — contains live virus.

-   MMR — contains live viruses.

-   Shingles (Shingrix) — although it contains no live virus, vaccination should be delayed.

### Timing

-   Anyone planning pregnancy should consult their provider beforehand, as some vaccinations should be completed before conception.

## Flu shot in pregnancy

-   The inactivated flu shot is safe at any stage of pregnancy, according to the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists.

-   Pregnancy changes the immune, cardiovascular and respiratory systems, increasing susceptibility to severe flu complications.

-   Maternal flu in early pregnancy may raise the risk of birth defects and congenital conditions.

-   Antibodies from maternal vaccination cross the placenta and appear in breast milk, protecting the infant until they are eligible for their own flu vaccine at six months.

-   Ask for the injected shot rather than the nasal spray, which contains live virus and is contraindicated.

-   Vaccination lowers the likelihood of flu-related hospitalisation in pregnancy.

-   Providers should discuss flu vaccination at prenatal appointments during flu season.

## X-ray during pregnancy: Is it safe?

-   The risk of fetal harm from X-rays is minimal, and diagnostic benefits typically outweigh potential dangers.

-   Low-risk: teeth, head, arms, legs and chest — minimal direct radiation to reproductive organs; lead aprons are not required.

-   Higher-risk: abdominal, lower back and pelvic X-rays deliver radiation directly to the fetus; risk depends on pregnancy stage, dose and imaging type.

-   Receiving radiation in very high doses during the first two weeks after conception could cause a miscarriage.

-   Very high doses in the first eight weeks may increase birth defect risk — restricted growth, smaller head size, skeletal or genital abnormalities.

-   High doses between 8 and 15 weeks may raise learning disability risk, though diagnostic doses are typically far below harmful thresholds.

-   Care teams can reduce fetal radiation through lower dosing and protective techniques if the X-ray can't be postponed.

-   Tell your provider before any X-ray so postponement or protection can be considered.

-   Don't hold children during their X-rays, to avoid incidental exposure.

# Part 7 — Lifestyle

## Working during pregnancy: Do's and don'ts

### Managing nausea and vomiting

-   Identify and sidestep workplace triggers such as odours or food smells.

-   Eat frequent small meals with bland options like crackers.

-   Consider over-the-counter vitamin B-6 for symptom relief.

-   Discuss prescription anti-nausea treatment with your provider if you're losing weight.

### Combating fatigue

-   Take periodic short breaks with minimal lighting and feet elevated.

-   Drink water early in the day rather than in the evening.

-   Walk or swim to boost energy.

-   Aim for eight or more hours of sleep a night, preferably on your side for circulation.

-   Eat iron-rich foods — leafy greens, fortified cereals, lean meats — to address potential anaemia.

### Workplace comfort

-   Use ergonomic seating with lumbar support, or add cushioning.

-   Stand at the desk periodically to reduce prolonged sitting strain.

-   Elevate swollen feet and legs through the day.

-   When standing for long periods, alternate feet on a footrest and wear supportive low-heeled shoes.

-   Lift by bending the knees rather than the waist, keeping loads close to the body.

### Reducing stress

-   Keep prioritised daily task lists and delegate where possible.

-   Share concerns with supportive colleagues or family.

-   Practise mindful breathing or meditation apps.

-   Explore prenatal yoga if your provider approves.

### Safety

-   Avoid harmful substances, radiation, loud noise and extreme temperatures.

-   Limit prolonged standing and heavy lifting without proper technique.

-   Minimise ladder climbing and night shift work where possible.

-   Consult your provider about necessary job duty modifications.

-   Keep recommended vaccinations up to date.

## Air travel during pregnancy: Is it safe?

### Timing

-   Before 36 weeks: generally safe for uncomplicated pregnancies.

-   After 36 weeks: providers typically advise against flying, and many airlines prohibit it.

-   The second trimester offers the lowest risk of pregnancy emergencies.

### Conditions where flying should be avoided

-   History of miscarriage or vaginal bleeding; severe anaemia; uncontrolled high blood pressure or diabetes; previous preeclampsia; multiple pregnancy.

### Airline policies

-   Some carriers restrict international flights for pregnant passengers.

-   Flights after 36 weeks may require a physician's letter confirming the stage of pregnancy and that flying is appropriate.

-   Check policies before booking.

### In-flight precautions

-   Fasten the seatbelt below the belly for the whole flight.

-   Drink fluids regularly — cabin humidity is low.

-   Avoid gas-producing foods and fizzy drinks before flying.

-   Walk the aisle hourly; flex the ankles when seated; wear compression stockings.

-   Avoid tight clothing that restricts circulation.

-   Carry copies of medical records and research obstetric care at your destination.

### Health considerations

-   Pregnancy increases the risk of venous thrombosis during air travel.

-   Radiation exposure is a minimal concern for occasional flyers — discuss with your provider if you fly frequently.

## Sex during pregnancy: What's OK, what's not

### Safety

-   Amniotic fluid and the uterine muscles protect the baby. Sex poses no risk to the fetus in an uncomplicated pregnancy, though comfort and desire may change.

### Miscarriage

-   Sex does not cause miscarriage — most losses result from abnormal fetal development.

-   Light cramping or spotting after sex or orgasm is normal; severe cramping or heavy period-like bleeding needs evaluation.

### Positions

-   Most positions remain appropriate if comfortable. As the abdomen expands, adjust based on comfort and preference.

### STI prevention

-   Partners with an active STI should avoid vaginal, oral and anal contact.

-   Condoms are recommended when either partner has multiple partners or when starting a new relationship during pregnancy.

### When to avoid

-   Vaginal bleeding; amniotic fluid leaking; cervical incompetence; placenta previa; a history of premature labour.

### Alternatives

-   Cuddling, kissing and massage provide connection without penetrative sex, for those uninterested due to fatigue, nausea or preference.

## Vaping during pregnancy

-   Using electronic cigarettes while pregnant is not safe.

-   E-cigarettes typically contain nicotine, which permanently damages a baby's developing brain and many other organs.

-   Vaping liquids also contain chemicals, flavours and additives that may pose risks to mother and baby.

-   Many pregnant people who vape believe e-cigarettes are less harmful than cigarettes, or don't realise the devices contain nicotine.

-   Some turn to vaping hoping it will help them quit or cut down on cigarettes.

-   Vaping may expose babies to fewer toxins than cigarettes, but nicotine exposure of any kind is harmful to a baby.

-   Quitting smoking or vaping entirely is the healthiest outcome. Discuss cessation options with your provider to access support.

## Falling during pregnancy: Reason to worry?

-   Early pregnancy falls are typically low-risk — the uterus sits behind the pelvic bone and is cushioned by amniotic fluid and thick muscular walls.

-   Falls later in pregnancy, after about 23 weeks, carry greater concern, particularly with impact on the abdomen, potentially causing contractions, fluid loss or placental separation.

-   Seek immediate medical evaluation for vaginal bleeding or fluid leakage, abdominal pain, contractions, or absence of fetal movement.

-   At 23 weeks and beyond, 24-hour hospital observation may be needed to rule out premature labour.

-   In severe third-trimester cases, emergency caesarean delivery may be required.

-   For a fall in the first or early second trimester, call your provider and describe symptoms in detail so they can assess the risk.

## Bed rest during pregnancy: Get the facts

### Does it prevent early labour?

-   There's no evidence that staying in bed during pregnancy, either at home or in the hospital, delays or stops preterm labour.

-   Bed rest is no longer routinely recommended despite its historical use.

### Alternative activity modifications

-   Selective restrictions may help those with a history of preterm labour or high premature birth risk.

-   Weight lifting limits under 20 pounds may be suggested.

-   Strenuous exercise such as strength training might need to stop.

-   Work adjustments: fewer than 40 hours weekly, avoiding night shifts, limiting prolonged standing.

-   Sex restrictions apply in specific cases: previous preterm labour or placenta previa.

### Risks of prolonged bed rest

-   Deep vein blood clots (venous thromboembolism).

-   Loss of bone density through demineralisation.

-   Cardiovascular and muscular deconditioning.

-   Psychological stress from childcare arrangements, employment uncertainty and lost income.

### Approach

-   Discuss recommendations thoroughly and consider whether targeted activity limits are a safer alternative to complete bed rest.

# Part 8 — Complications and existing conditions

## High-risk pregnancy: Know what to expect

### Definition

-   A pregnancy where the pregnant person or fetus faces an elevated likelihood of health complications before, during or after delivery, requiring extra monitoring and testing.

### Risk factors

-   Age: under 20 or over 35.

-   Lifestyle: alcohol, tobacco, vaping and illicit drug use.

-   Pre-existing conditions: hypertension, obesity, diabetes, epilepsy, thyroid disorders, cardiac or blood vessel disease, uncontrolled asthma, infections.

-   Pregnancy-specific: abnormal placental positioning, severe fetal growth restriction, Rh incompatibility.

-   Multiple gestations — assisted reproductive technologies may raise certain complications.

-   Previous history: preeclampsia, premature delivery, genetic abnormalities, miscarriage or stillbirth may recur.

### Preventive steps

-   Preconception appointments to discuss medications, vaccinations, genetic risks and lifestyle.

-   Regular prenatal care with potential specialist referrals.

-   Eliminate tobacco; avoid alcohol and drugs.

### Diagnostic testing

-   Ultrasound variants: 3D/4D imaging for developmental abnormalities; cervical length measurement for premature labour risk; biophysical profiles for fetal wellness.

-   Cell-free DNA testing examines fetal chromosomes via a maternal blood sample.

-   Amniocentesis (after 15 weeks) and chorionic villus sampling (10–13 weeks) confirm genetic conditions and neural tube defects.

-   Blood and urine tests screen for infections, anaemia, diabetes and genetic markers.

### Warning signs needing immediate attention

-   Vaginal bleeding or unusual discharge.

-   Abdominal or pelvic pain or cramping.

-   Decreased fetal movement.

-   Severe headaches, vision changes or facial swelling.

-   Fever, urinary symptoms, chest discomfort or breathing difficulty.

-   Extreme fatigue, dizziness or weakness.

-   Mental health concerns including persistent sadness or thoughts of self-harm.

## High blood pressure and pregnancy: Know the facts

### Types

-   Chronic hypertension: develops before conception or within the first 20 weeks; may be symptomless, so onset timing can be unclear.

-   Chronic hypertension with superimposed preeclampsia: existing hypertension worsens during pregnancy, potentially causing protein in the urine or organ complications.

-   Gestational hypertension: high blood pressure that develops after 20 weeks of pregnancy without protein in urine or organ damage; may progress to preeclampsia.

-   Preeclampsia: develops after 20 weeks with signs of organ damage (kidneys, liver, blood, brain). Untreated it can cause eclampsia with seizures.

### Risks

-   Placental insufficiency: reduced blood flow limits oxygen and nutrients, causing growth restriction, low birth weight or premature delivery.

-   Placental abruption: premature separation from the uterine wall, which can cause severe bleeding threatening both.

-   Fetal growth problems.

-   Organ damage: poorly controlled high blood pressure can injure the brain, eyes, heart, lungs, kidneys and liver.

-   Prematurity complications: respiratory distress, infection and others.

-   Long-term: a history of preeclampsia increases future cardiovascular disease risk, especially with recurrence or premature delivery.

### Preeclampsia warning signs

-   Severe headaches; vision changes (temporary loss, blurring, light sensitivity); upper abdominal pain beneath the right ribcage; nausea or vomiting; low platelets or impaired liver function; shortness of breath from fluid in the lungs; excessive swelling of face and hands beyond typical pregnancy oedema.

### Blood pressure categories

-   Elevated: systolic 120–129, diastolic below 80 mm Hg.

-   Stage 1: systolic 130–139 or diastolic 80–89 mm Hg.

-   Stage 2: systolic 140 or above, or diastolic 90 or above.

-   Gestational hypertension threshold: above 140/90 after 20 weeks without organ damage, documented twice at least 4 hours apart.

### Medication

-   Safe options exist, but ACE inhibitors, angiotensin II receptor blockers and renin inhibitors need to be avoided.

-   Low-dose aspirin (81 mg daily) is often recommended for high-risk individuals starting late in the first trimester.

-   Don't stop taking medication or change the dose on your own.

### Preconception and monitoring

-   See specialists experienced in hypertensive pregnancy complications; coordinate with your primary care provider or cardiologist; consider treatment changes before conception; reach a healthy weight if overweight.

-   Frequent appointments with blood pressure and weight checks, regular lab testing, serial ultrasounds for fetal growth, fetal well-being assessments and movement tracking.

### Risk reduction

-   Attend all prenatal care; take prescribed medicines and aspirin consistently; follow activity recommendations; eat a balanced diet (a dietitian can help); avoid smoking, alcohol and illegal substances; discuss over-the-counter medicines with your provider.

### Labour, delivery and postpartum

-   Early induction may be recommended depending on blood pressure control and organ damage severity.

-   Seizure prevention medication may be given during labour for worsening preeclampsia.

-   Delivery timing depends on fetal complications such as slow growth and on maternal stability.

-   Breastfeeding is encouraged even while on antihypertensive medication — discuss medication safety before delivery.

## Heart conditions and pregnancy: Know the risks

### Physiological impact

-   Blood volume expands 30–50% to support fetal development; cardiac output rises with a faster heart rate. Labour and delivery add to the heart's workload too. Recovery happens gradually over weeks after delivery.

### Major risk categories

-   Arrhythmias: abnormal rates may feel fast, slow or irregular, and create vulnerability to clots that raise stroke and cardiac risk.

-   Valve dysfunction: scarring or structural defects impair circulation. Mechanical valves need careful blood-thinner management. Endocarditis is a serious concern requiring antibiotic prophylaxis.

-   Heart failure: reduced pumping efficiency worsens as fluid demands escalate.

-   Congenital heart defects: maternal conditions increase the fetal risk of similar abnormalities.

### Conditions where pregnancy should be avoided

-   Advanced heart failure, peripartum cardiomyopathy, critical valve stenosis, coarctation of the aorta, Eisenmenger syndrome, certain Marfan presentations and pulmonary hypertension — because maternal mortality risk is very high.

### Medication and preparation

-   Prescribed cardiac medicines generally remain necessary; benefits often outweigh the risks. Providers adjust doses and choose pregnancy-compatible alternatives.

-   Coordinate care between a cardiologist and a maternal-fetal medicine specialist before conception.

### Monitoring and lifestyle

-   Regular visits with weight and blood pressure checks, periodic lab work, echocardiograms and electrocardiograms.

-   Attend all appointments; take medicines exactly as prescribed; prioritise rest and avoid strenuous exertion; keep to weight gain targets; manage stress; eliminate smoking, alcohol and drugs; limit caffeine.

### Labour and delivery

-   Vaginal delivery at a high-risk facility is typical for controlled conditions, with continuous cardiac and fetal monitoring.

-   Positioning may restrict lying flat; epidural or spinal anaesthesia is often recommended; assisted delivery may limit pushing.

-   C-sections are reserved for specific complications. Antibiotic prophylaxis is given for endocarditis risk.

### Breastfeeding

-   Encouraged for most cardiac patients on medication; those with severe endocarditis risk might need to bottle-feed to prevent mastitis complications.

## Pregnancy and atrial fibrillation

-   Atrial fibrillation may worsen during pregnancy or appear for the first time. It raises the risk of clots in the heart, while pregnancy itself raises clot risk in the legs. Coordinating heart and obstetric teams is essential.

-   Avoid warfarin. Direct oral anticoagulants — dabigatran, rivaroxaban, edoxaban, apixaban — lack sufficient pregnancy safety research and are not recommended.

-   Heparin and low molecular weight heparin, such as enoxaparin (Lovenox), can be used with close monitoring, as they do not cross the placenta. Many pregnant people with AFib receive low molecular weight heparin injections.

-   Warning signs needing immediate care: chest pain, shortness of breath, leg swelling. Emergency stroke symptoms: changes in vision, trouble speaking, numbness in the face, arms or legs.

-   Anticoagulants are typically stopped during labour and delivery unless clot risk is very high, then usually resumed afterwards. Some are unsuitable while breastfeeding — discuss alternatives.

## Pregnancy and obesity: Know the risks

### Measurement

-   BMI of 30 or higher defines obesity — weight divided by height squared. Asian populations may face increased risk at a BMI of 23 or above.

### Fertility

-   Elevated BMI can reduce the chance of conception by disrupting ovulation. Higher BMI correlates with longer time to conceive, even with regular ovulation. IVF success rates may also decline.

### Maternal risks

-   Pregnancy loss through miscarriage or stillbirth, including recurrent loss; gestational diabetes; preeclampsia with organ damage; cardiac complications; obstructive sleep apnoea; caesarean delivery and wound infections.

### Fetal and childhood effects

-   Restricted fetal development; birth defects; excessive birth weight; childhood asthma and obesity; cognitive and developmental delays.

### Weight gain guidelines

-   Single pregnancy with BMI 30 or above: 11–20 pounds. Multiples with BMI 30 or above: 25–42 pounds. Individual targets vary.

### Specialised monitoring

-   Early gestational diabetes screening at the first visit; additional ultrasounds to compensate for imaging limitations; obstructive sleep apnoea evaluation and specialist referral if needed.

### Supportive actions

-   Preconception counselling; consistent prenatal care; balanced nutrition with dietitian guidance; approved physical activity such as walking or swimming; avoiding tobacco, alcohol and illegal substances.

## Epilepsy and pregnancy: What you need to know

### Fertility

-   Epilepsy itself does not impair the ability to conceive, but some anti-seizure medicines may reduce fertility or lower the effectiveness of hormonal birth control.

### Risks from seizures

-   Seizures can slow the fetal heart rate and reduce oxygen delivery. Complications include premature labour, low birth weight and early delivery. Trauma from falls may cause placental separation or fetal loss. Severity depends on seizure type and frequency.

### Seizure frequency in pregnancy

-   Most people experience stable or reduced frequency. Some experience more, particularly with sleep deprivation or missed doses.

### Medication

-   Anti-seizure drugs carry risks of birth defects including cleft palate and heart problems; risk increases with higher doses and multiple medicines.

-   Generally, continuing medication is safer than uncontrolled seizures. Providers prescribe the lowest effective dose and may monitor levels through pregnancy.

### Preparation

-   Consult obstetric and neurological specialists before conception; get epilepsy well-controlled first; take medication exactly as prescribed; eat well, sleep enough, take prenatal vitamins; avoid caffeine, cigarettes, alcohol and illegal substances.

### Folic acid

-   Critical for preventing neural tube defects. Recommended 0.4 milligrams of folic acid daily in prenatal vitamins; higher doses may be needed. Begin three months before conception.

### Monitoring

-   Frequent visits with blood pressure and weight checks, blood tests to monitor medication levels, frequent ultrasounds. Vitamin K may be recommended near delivery to prevent infant bleeding complications.

### Seizures during pregnancy

-   Many deliver healthy babies despite seizure activity. Report any seizure immediately; medication adjustments may be needed; hospital monitoring may follow a seizure in the final trimester.

### Labour, delivery and after

-   Most deliver without complications; seizures during labour are uncommon. Intravenous medication can stop a seizure in labour; a prolonged seizure may require caesarean delivery. Doses typically return to pre-pregnancy levels after birth.

-   Breastfeeding is generally encouraged even on anti-seizure medication; timing doses after feeds may be suggested.

## Rheumatoid arthritis: Does pregnancy affect symptoms?

-   Many people with rheumatoid arthritis experience notable relief during pregnancy, typically between the fourth and sixth months — though this is not universal, and some worsen.

-   Those who improve frequently flare after delivery, most commonly within the first three months. The reason remains under investigation.

-   Sex hormones may play a role, given that people assigned female at birth develop RA more often. But oestrogen-containing medicines such as oral contraceptives or hormone replacement typically don't alter symptoms, suggesting the relationship is more complex.

-   Immune changes during pregnancy prevent rejection of the fetus; researchers are examining whether these shifts explain symptom improvement.

-   Certain medications used to treat rheumatoid arthritis are not safe for use during pregnancy and need to be stopped before conception. Work with your provider on a management plan before and throughout pregnancy.

## How do ankylosing spondylitis and pregnancy affect each other?

-   The condition does not appear to impair the ability to conceive.

-   There may be elevated risks of premature delivery or reduced birth weight, though many deliver healthy infants.

-   Findings on symptoms are mixed — some evidence suggests pain and spinal stiffness improve, other studies show minimal change. Discomfort may intensify late in pregnancy from the added physical stress.

-   Flare-ups commonly follow childbirth, so a strong support network helps during recovery.

-   NSAIDs are typically unsafe during pregnancy, but certain biologic treatments may be appropriate to maintain control.

-   Consult a rheumatologist before conception to discuss treatment changes, and seek referral to a provider experienced in managing pregnancy with ankylosing spondylitis.

## Pregnancy and COVID-19: What are the risks?

### Illness during pregnancy

-   Pregnant individuals contract the virus at similar rates to others; most recover without hospitalisation, but pregnancy raises the risk of severe illness.

-   Risk stays elevated for at least one month postpartum.

-   Obesity, diabetes, hypertension and lung disease compound severity.

-   Severe cases may cause heart damage, blood clots, kidney injury, preterm birth and preeclampsia.

### Vaccination and prevention

-   Current US vaccines include Moderna, Pfizer-BioNTech and Novavax.

-   Vaccination is recommended during pregnancy and when planning pregnancy, and may protect newborns from infection.

-   Prevention: test after exposure, hand hygiene, masking in crowded areas, distance from people who are ill.

### Prenatal care

-   In-person visits typically continue as normal.

-   A positive test warrants discussion about monitoring options. Infection rarely transmits to the fetus.

-   Symptomatic patients are monitored closely and may receive antiviral medicines. Additional imaging may follow infection to confirm fetal development.

### Labour, delivery and postpartum

-   A positive status near delivery may not require birth plan changes, though providers may adjust timing or delivery method based on individual risk.

-   Babies may safely remain with infected mothers with proper precautions — masks and hand hygiene minimise transmission.

### Breastfeeding

-   Safe to continue if you feel well enough. Wash hands before nursing and wear a mask. Pumped milk requires proper equipment sanitation and hand washing.

## Chickenpox and pregnancy: What are the concerns?

### Risks during pregnancy

-   Increased danger of pneumonia, which can be more severe than in non-pregnant people.

-   Infection in the first 20 weeks risks congenital varicella syndrome, a rare group of serious birth defects; the highest risk window is weeks 8–20.

-   Congenital varicella syndrome may cause skin scarring and permanent damage to eyes, brain, limbs and digestive system.

### Risks near delivery

-   Chickenpox shortly before or during delivery can cause neonatal varicella, which can be life-threatening in a baby's first weeks.

### Prevention and testing

-   A blood test can determine immunity status.

-   If not immune and exposed, contact your provider immediately; immune globulin within 10 days of exposure may reduce infection risk and severity.

### Treatment

-   Antiviral medicines are most effective when started within 24 hours of the rash appearing.

-   Newborns of infected mothers may receive immune globulin shortly after birth, and antiviral medicine treats chickenpox in infants.

### Before pregnancy

-   Non-immune individuals should be vaccinated before pregnancy, waiting at least one month after the second dose before conceiving.

## Yeast infection during pregnancy: Over-the-counter treatment OK?

-   Non-prescription antifungal vaginal products are considered safe throughout pregnancy and don't cause birth defects or complications.

-   Clotrimazole and miconazole are the main over-the-counter options, as creams, ointments and suppositories — seven-day formulas are preferred for best results.

-   Hormonal fluctuations alter vaginal pH, making yeast infections more common in pregnancy.

-   Typical symptoms: vaginal itching with a white, thick discharge that looks like cottage cheese.

-   Topical treatments are recommended over oral medicines, due to possible associations between oral antifungals and miscarriage or birth defects.

-   Avoid fluconazole and other prescription oral antifungals, particularly in the first trimester.

-   Confirm symptoms with a provider before starting treatment, and disclose pregnancy when seeking care.

## Cervical length: Why does it matter during pregnancy?

### Why it matters

-   The cervix is the lower part of the uterus connecting to the vagina. Premature shortening raises the risk of preterm labour — after week 20 and before week 37. Earlier preterm births carry greater risk for the baby.

### Normal changes

-   Before pregnancy the cervix is closed, long and firm; during pregnancy it gradually softens; just before labour it shortens; finally it opens.

### Detection

-   Shortening usually happens without noticeable symptoms and is typically found on routine ultrasound. It may sometimes accompany preterm labour symptoms.

### Warning symptoms

-   Frequent consistent contractions; persistent low back discomfort; cramping; changes in vaginal discharge; pelvic pressure.

### Measurements and interventions

-   Less than 29 mm but more than 25 mm: additional monitoring ultrasounds.

-   Less than 25 mm before 24 weeks: cervical cerclage may be considered, using stitches or tape to keep the cervix closed.

-   Progesterone treatment may reduce premature birth risk for certain candidates.

### Progesterone eligibility

-   Cervical length 25 mm or less; pregnancy before 25 weeks; no preterm labour symptoms; a single pregnancy, not multiples; no previous preterm birth before 37 weeks.

### Activity restrictions

-   Bed rest and activity limits lack evidence for preventing preterm birth, and most providers no longer recommend them.

## Low amniotic fluid: Can it be treated?

-   Oligohydramnios is when amniotic fluid falls below the expected range for the baby's stage — a serious complication.

-   The fluid cushions the baby, prevents cord compression, allows movement and development, and reflects the baby's urine output as a wellness indicator.

-   There's no treatment that can completely correct this condition, though temporary interventions exist.

-   Causes include ruptured membranes, placental separation, maternal conditions such as hypertension or diabetes, certain medicines (ACE inhibitors), and fetal abnormalities.

-   Before 36 weeks: increased fluid intake, IV hydration in hospital if needed, and regular ultrasound monitoring.

-   At 36–37 weeks and beyond: delivery is generally the safest course.

-   During labour, amnioinfusion — introducing saline into the uterus via catheter — may be performed if fetal heart rate problems emerge.

-   Decisions depend on the underlying cause, maternal health, fetal age and the baby's condition.

# Part 9 — Mental health and pregnancy loss

## Depression during pregnancy: You're not alone

### Prevalence and detection

-   Around 7–9% of pregnant people in high-income countries experience depression during pregnancy, with potentially higher rates elsewhere.

-   Providers often fail to recognise it compared with non-pregnant populations, because pregnancy symptoms mask the indicators.

-   Cultural stigma and a focus on physical rather than mental wellbeing contribute to underdiagnosis.

### Risk factors

-   Anxiety and significant life stress; previous personal or family history of depression; stopping prescribed antidepressants; limited social support; unplanned pregnancy; domestic violence or prior abuse; pregnancy complications; prolonged nausea, vomiting or illness; history of PMS or premenstrual dysphoric disorder; sleep disturbance; young maternal age.

### Pregnancy-specific warning signs

-   Excessive worry about the baby; doubts about parenting capability; disinterest in the pregnancy; resistance to support from loved ones or providers; neglecting prenatal care; substance use; inadequate weight gain from poor nutrition.

### General symptoms lasting two weeks or more

-   Persistent sadness most days; loss of enjoyment in previously enjoyed activities; guilt, hopelessness or worthlessness; sleep disruption or excessive sleeping; significant appetite changes affecting weight; fatigue and low energy; difficulty concentrating; agitation; suicidal thoughts, which require immediate emergency intervention.

### Why treatment matters

-   Untreated depression may result in missed prenatal care and poor nutrition affecting fetal development, significantly raises the risk of postpartum depression, and may compromise mother-infant bonding.

### Treatment and screening

-   Options: psychotherapy or talk therapy, antidepressant medication, or a combination.

-   The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists recommends screening at minimum once during pregnancy — typically at the initial prenatal visit and again in the third trimester.

## Miscarriage and stress

-   Between 10 and 20% of recognised pregnancies end in miscarriage, and the actual rate is likely higher since many occur before pregnancy is detected.

-   Most early miscarriages stem from chromosomal abnormalities disrupting embryonic development, not from anything the pregnant person did.

-   Brief, manageable stress such as a work deadline doesn't raise miscarriage risk, but chronic or acute severe stress may.

-   Higher-risk stress includes prolonged financial hardship, bereavement, housing instability and other ongoing hardship.

-   Chronic stress raises cortisol, potentially triggering immune changes, glucose metabolism disruption and greater infection vulnerability — all linked to pregnancy loss.

-   Anyone experiencing significant ongoing or sudden stress should consult their provider for resources and support.

-   Managing existing conditions such as diabetes and thyroid disease, maintaining a healthy weight, and avoiding smoking, alcohol and drugs may lower miscarriage risk.

## Blighted ovum: What causes it?

-   An anembryonic pregnancy — the older term is "blighted ovum" — is a pregnancy where an embryo never develops or stops forming and is reabsorbed.

-   The exact reason is largely unknown, though chromosomal problems in the fertilised egg may be responsible.

-   These pregnancies typically end early, sometimes before a person realises they are pregnant, though pregnancy tests may detect hCG.

-   The placenta begins forming briefly, which is why hCG is produced and detected.

-   Early symptoms can include typical signs like tender breasts, nausea and vomiting.

-   As hormone levels drop, symptoms fade and may be replaced by mild cramping, pelvic pain, light spotting or vaginal bleeding.

-   Diagnosis: ultrasound reveals an empty gestational sac with no embryo.

-   Resolution: the miscarriage may occur naturally, be triggered with medication, or be managed by dilation and curettage.

-   Most people who experience this go on to have successful pregnancies; recurring losses warrant medical evaluation.

## Placenta: How it works, what's normal

### Function

-   A temporary organ that develops during pregnancy and attaches to the uterine wall via the umbilical cord, delivering oxygen and nutrients to the fetus and removing waste from fetal blood.

### Attachment

-   Typically at the top, side, front or back of the uterus. Uncommon low positioning near the cervix may mean the placenta blocks the passage that connects the uterus to the vagina.

### Risk factors for placental problems

-   Advanced maternal age, particularly after 40; premature rupture of membranes; hypertension; multiple pregnancy; coagulation disorders; previous uterine procedures such as C-section or fibroid removal; history of prior placental conditions; tobacco or cocaine use; abdominal trauma from accidents or falls.

### Common disorders

-   Placental abruption: premature detachment deprives the fetus of oxygen and nutrients. Abdominal or back pain with vaginal bleeding; may require emergency early delivery.

-   Placenta previa: the placenta partially or completely covers the cervical opening. Often resolves naturally in early pregnancy, but persistent cases can cause severe haemorrhage in labour and typically require caesarean delivery.

-   Placenta accreta: abnormal invasion into the uterine muscle prevents normal separation after birth, raising haemorrhage risk and often requiring hysterectomy after caesarean delivery.

-   Retained placenta: failure to deliver the placenta within 30 minutes postpartum, from cervical entrapment or incomplete detachment. Without intervention this creates serious infection and bleeding risk.

### Warning symptoms

-   Heavy vaginal bleeding, abdominal discomfort, lower back pain, or contractions during pregnancy.

### Risk reduction

-   Most placental complications can't be directly prevented, but regular prenatal visits, managing conditions such as hypertension, avoiding tobacco and drugs, and discussing caesarean considerations beforehand all help.

### Delivery

-   Vaginal delivery includes a third stage in which the placenta is expelled; providers may give oxytocin to promote contractions and massage the abdomen to encourage release. In caesarean delivery the placenta is removed surgically.

# Part 10 — Late pregnancy and multiples

## Overdue pregnancy: What to do when baby's overdue

### What counts as overdue

-   A due date estimates when pregnancy reaches 40 weeks, not a guaranteed delivery date. Pregnancies are classed as "postterm" only when they extend two weeks beyond it. Births before or after the due date are normal variation.

### Risk factors

-   First-time pregnancies; a previous postterm pregnancy; a male fetus; maternal obesity (BMI 30 or higher); incorrectly calculated due dates (from an uncertain last period or ultrasounds after 22 weeks); possible genetic predisposition or, rarely, placental or fetal complications.

### Risks for the baby

-   Larger than average birth size, potentially needing forceps, vacuum assistance or caesarean delivery.

-   Postmaturity syndrome: decreased skin fat, absent protective coating, reduced fine body hair and meconium staining.

-   Insufficient amniotic fluid affecting heart rate and potentially compressing the cord.

### Risks for the mother

-   Severe vaginal tearing, infection, and excessive postpartum bleeding.

### Monitoring

-   Testing typically starts when pregnancy extends beyond one week past the due date: nonstress tests to evaluate the baby's heart rate, and biophysical profiles examining cardiac rhythm, breathing, muscle tone and movement. Amniotic fluid levels are also assessed.

### Induction techniques

-   Cervical ripening: medication softens cervical tissue, or a catheter with an inflatable balloon mechanically expands the cervix.

-   Membrane stripping: a gloved finger separates the amniotic sac lining from the surrounding uterine tissue.

-   Rupturing the amniotic sac: a thin plastic hook creates an opening to release fluid.

-   Medication-induced contractions: synthetic oxytocin (Pitocin).

-   These procedures typically happen in hospital labour and delivery units.

## Twin pregnancy: Getting ready for twins or multiples

### How multiples develop

-   Fraternal twins: two separate eggs fertilised by two sperm. Each develops in its own amniotic and chorionic sac with separate placental tissue. Can be the same or different sexes. The most common type.

-   Identical twins: one fertilised egg divides early. May share a placenta, or share placental tissue with separate sacs. Identical genetic material, always the same sex with matching physical traits. Rarely they develop as conjoined twins.

### Detection

-   Usually identified on ultrasound. Occasionally a pregnancy that initially appears to involve multiples resolves into a single pregnancy — "vanishing twin syndrome" — which can cause emotional distress.

### Maternal care

-   Frequent prenatal visits to assess fetal development and maternal health; regular screening for signs of premature labour; multiple ultrasounds and diagnostic testing as pregnancy advances.

-   Weight gain: 37–54 pounds for those at a healthy pre-pregnancy weight; typically about 600 extra calories daily. Discuss individual needs with your provider.

-   Induction may be recommended in the late third trimester if labour doesn't start spontaneously; caesarean delivery may be necessary.

### Complications

-   Premature birth: likelihood increases with the number of fetuses. Steroids are given to accelerate lung maturation. Newborns may have breathing difficulties, feeding challenges, vision issues and infections.

-   Gestational diabetes: your risk of diabetes that starts during pregnancy is higher with multiples. Managed with blood sugar monitoring and dietary guidance; may need endocrinologist and nutritionist input.

-   Hypertension disorders: elevated risk. Preeclampsia can damage organs including kidneys and liver, and may necessitate early delivery.

-   Twin-twin transfusion: specific to identical twins sharing placental circulation — one twin receives excessive blood and the other insufficient, creating serious cardiac complications that require prenatal intervention.

-   Delivery method: vaginal delivery is possible if the first twin is head-down; complications may require caesarean delivery for the second twin. Three or more typically require surgical delivery.

### Postpartum

-   Newborns need standard infant care but parents need more rest and help. Increased risk of postpartum depression means professional support and community aid matter.
